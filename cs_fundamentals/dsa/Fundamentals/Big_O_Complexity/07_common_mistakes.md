## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls in Interviews

This section outlines common errors, anti-patterns, and pitfalls to avoid during coding interviews, focusing on both technical execution and communication.

### 1. Jumping into Code Too Soon
- **Mistake**: Not taking enough time to understand the problem, clarify constraints, and plan an approach.
- **Why it's bad**: Leads to writing incorrect code, hitting edge cases you didn't consider, and having to backtrack significantly.
- **Solution**: Always follow the interview stages: clarify, example, plan, then code. Think out loud!

### 2. Not Communicating Your Thought Process
- **Mistake**: Coding silently.
- **Why it's bad**: The interviewer can't evaluate your problem-solving skills if they don't know what you're thinking. They might assume you're stuck or don't know what you're doing.
- **Solution**: Think out loud constantly. Explain your decisions, assumptions, and trade-offs. If stuck, verbalize your struggle and potential next steps.

### 3. Ignoring Edge Cases
- **Mistake**: Only considering the "happy path" (typical inputs) and forgetting about empty inputs, single-element inputs, nulls, maximum/minimum values, etc.
- **Why it's bad**: Leads to bugs and shows a lack of thoroughness.
- **Solution**: Proactively ask about edge cases during the clarification phase. Test with them after you've written your code.

### 4. Over-Optimizing Prematurely
- **Mistake**: Jumping straight to the most complex, optimized solution without first considering simpler, brute-force approaches.
- **Why it's bad**: You might get stuck, waste time on an unnecessary optimization, or write overly complicated code that's hard to debug.
- **Solution**: Start with a brute-force approach, discuss its complexity, and then incrementally optimize. This demonstrates a solid problem-solving process.

### 5. Not Testing Your Code
- **Mistake**: Finishing coding and saying "I'm done."
- **Why it's bad**: You're almost guaranteed to have subtle bugs. Interviewers want to see your debugging process.
- **Solution**: Manually walk through your code with an example, tracking variable states. Use the test cases you discussed earlier.

### 6. Poor Code Readability
- **Mistake**: Using cryptic variable names, inconsistent formatting, or spaghetti code.
- **Why it's bad**: Makes your code hard to understand and signals that you might not be a good collaborator.
- **Solution**: Use meaningful names, break down complex logic into helper functions, and adhere to standard formatting.

### 7. Incorrect Complexity Analysis
- **Mistake**: Stating incorrect time or space complexity, or not being able to justify your answer.
- **Why it's bad**: Shows a fundamental lack of understanding of algorithm efficiency.
- **Solution**: Practice analyzing complexity for every problem you solve. Understand *why* an operation is O(1) or O(log n).

### 8. Not Asking Questions
- **Mistake**: Not asking the interviewer questions at the end of the interview.
- **Why it's bad**: Implies a lack of interest in the role or the company.
- **Solution**: Always have 2-3 thoughtful questions prepared about the team, culture, tech stack, or the interviewer's experience.

Avoiding these common pitfalls will significantly improve your performance and leave a strong positive impression.
